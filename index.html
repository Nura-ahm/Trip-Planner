Constraint-based itinerary planning

Five days.
Forty attractions.
One sensible order.

Tell it where you are going, how long for and what you like. It scores every place in the city against your interests, fits what your budget and your hours allow, groups the result into days that stay in one neighbourhood, and puts each day in the order that walks the least.

It runs entirely in your browser — no account, no key, no waiting on a server — and it shows its working, so you can see what it optimised rather than take the itinerary on trust.

03 — Method

Seven passes

Planning a trip is a scheduling problem with four constraints pulling against each other: what you want to see, what a day physically holds, what is open when, and what you can spend. Each pass narrows the problem for the next one.

  1. Score

    Every place in the city is rated against your interests, its own standing, and what it does to a day's budget. Interests dominate — a plan full of excellent places you did not ask for is a bad plan.

  2. Select

    A bounded knapsack: maximise total score inside a time budget and a money budget. Solved greedily by score-per-minute, then a swap pass that fixes what greedy gets wrong.

  3. Cluster

    k-means over the coordinates, one cluster per day, seeded furthest-point-first. Then a balancing pass, because k-means optimises for tightness and will happily give you a nine-stop day and a one-stop day.

  4. Route

    Each day is an open travelling-salesman problem. Nearest neighbour from every possible starting stop, each result polished with 2-opt, shortest one kept — plus your own shortlist order in the same competition, so the result can never be worse than the list it started from.

  5. Schedule

    The route is laid against the clock with real opening hours and travel times. Anything that will not fit is offered to every other day at every position, and only dropped if nowhere takes it.

  6. Top up

    Selection works on estimates, so the finished schedule usually leaves gaps. This inserts the best remaining places wherever the detour is smallest, favouring days that still have room in them.

  7. Improve

    Hill climbing: move a stop to another day, keep the move if the trip gets shorter without losing a stop, stop when a full sweep finds nothing. Then trim to the budget, even out the days, and re-route each one a final time.

04 — About

Good to know

  • Curated, not scraped. Five cities, each catalogued by hand with coordinates, typical opening hours and indicative prices — enough to plan a real day around. Hours and prices do shift with the season, so check before you buy a ticket.
  • Classical AI, not a chatbot. Scoring, clustering, a travelling-salesman search and hill climbing. Building an itinerary is a scheduling problem, and that is what algorithms are for.
  • Distances are as the crow flies. Real streets run a little longer, though every route on the page is measured the same way, so the comparisons hold.
  • Nothing leaves your browser. No server, no account, no analytics. Saved trips live in this browser's own storage and nowhere else.

Built with

Vanilla JavaScript, no framework and no build step. Three files: the catalogue, the engine, and the interface.

planner.js contains no DOM code at all, so the interesting half of the project can be tested on its own — and is, across every city, length, pace and budget combination.
